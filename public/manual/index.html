IN THE MOUNTAINS

A deep tactical-strategic simulation of counterinsurgency at the platoon/company level, in a procedurally generated valley of Kunar Province, Afghanistan, c. 2011.

You are the commander of a remote combat outpost. The men, the equipment, the patrols, the shuras with the elders, the calls for fire, the wounded — they are yours. The valley is steep, the enemy fights on his own ground and his own timeline, and the population is the prize. This manual is your reference. Read it, or learn the hard way; the valley teaches both.

Tone & Sources
The simulation draws on Sebastian Junger's War, the documentaries Restrepo and Korengal (Junger / Tim Hetherington), Jake Tapper's The Outpost, counterinsurgency doctrine (FM 3-24), and the after-action record of units that fought in the Pech and Korengal. It is a work of fiction made in respect of those who served there.

1. The Situation

It is 2011. Your company has inherited a combat outpost (COP) on a knob of high ground above a river valley. North, the valley climbs into "Indian country" — switchback trails, draws choked with holly and cedar, ridgelines at nearly 3,000 meters. A handful of villages cling to benches above the river, growing corn and timber and, when the season is right, fighting.

Your enemy — call them ACM (Anti-Coalition Militia), the Taliban, HIG, outsiders, local boys with a grievance and an AK — does not mass and charge. He ambushes from defilade, fires a few magazines, and melts up the draws before your mortars can range him. He is patient. He is home. The terrain is his ally and your enemy in equal measure.

Your weapons are superior; your firepower is overwhelming. And almost none of that matters if you cannot win the people. Every civilian you kill makes ten fighters. Every promise you break is remembered for a generation. You can win every firefight and lose the valley.

2. The Two Layers

Command Layer

Turn-based, resolved by phase of day. Manage the COP: personnel, logistics, defense, intelligence. Plan and launch patrols and operations on the topographic map. Engage the population. Answer to battalion.

Contact Layer

Real-time-with-pause tactical combat. Triggered when a patrol makes contact, when the COP is attacked, or when you spring an ambush. Squad and individual control, with every round simulated against line of sight, cover, and concealment.

3. The Game Loop

  1. Plan — at the COP, study intel, pick a mission, task-organize an element, draw a route.
  2. Patrol — step off. Most patrols are long, hot, and uneventful. Some are not.
  3. Fight — on contact, drop into the tactical layer. Maneuver, suppress, call for fire, treat the wounded, break contact or destroy the enemy.
  4. Manage — between phases, rest your men, run shuras, fund projects, resupply, and respond to events.
  5. Repeat — for an entire deployment. The valley keeps score.

4. The COP (Command View)

The command bar across the top shows the day and tour length, the phase of day (Dawn → Day → Dusk → Night), the light level (which governs night-vision advantage), the weather (which governs air support), and your five campaign metrics. The center is the topographic map. The left column holds directives, intel, and the log; the right column holds patrol planning, village engagement, and logistics.

Press Advance to move time forward by one phase. Your men rest and recover at the COP, supplies are consumed, intel arrives, and the enemy moves. The night belongs to him.

5. Personnel

Your platoon is roughly 35 soldiers in a headquarters element, three rifle squads, and a weapons squad. Each soldier is an individual with a name, a hometown, a rank, a job, and a set of attributes that actually drive the simulation:

  • Marksmanship tightens shot dispersion.
  • Composure resists suppression and panic.
  • Leadership steadies nearby soldiers under fire.
  • Medical determines how fast a casualty is stabilized.
  • Fitness resists fatigue on the brutal slopes.
  • Experience improves perception and steadiness.

Readiness is shown by a colored dot: green = ready, amber = resting, red = wounded. Wounded soldiers recover over days; the killed do not come back. Their loss costs you morale across the platoon and confidence at battalion. Bring a medic (DOC) on every patrol.

6. Logistics

Everything is finite and everything is heavy. The Logistics panel tracks ammunition by caliber (5.56, 7.62, .50), 40mm and mortar rounds, grenades and smoke, trauma kits, water, food, fuel, and batteries. Firefights and fire missions burn through stocks fast. Resupply comes by ground convoy (risk of IED/ambush) or by air (weather permitting) — usually offered as an event.

Watch your basic load
Bars turn red when a class of supply runs low. Black-on-ammo in a firefight means your guns fall silent. A unit that runs dry will switch to a sidearm or stop firing entirely.

7. Planning Patrols

Patrol planning lives in the right-hand panel and on the map:

  1. Mission — choose the type. Each shapes the encounter and the rewards:
    • Presence show the flag; visiting villages raises attitudes.
    • Recon gather atmospherics and intel on an area.
    • Ambush you set up first and initiate on your terms.
    • KLE reach a village to meet its elder.
    • Census log military-age males; build the human terrain.
    • Cordon & Search presence with teeth.
    • Overwatch / OP sit on high ground and watch a route.
  2. Task-organize — click soldiers, or a squad header to toggle the whole squad. Mind your composition.
  3. Route — with Plan Route selected, click the map to drop waypoints. Distance is shown. Deeper into the valley means more danger.
  4. Step Off — launch. The patrol marches; contact probability is rolled along the route from terrain, enemy activity, village hostility, and intel hotspots.

8. The People (Counterinsurgency)

Switch the map to Inspect and click a village. Each has an elder, a population, an attitude toward you, a cooperation level (how much intel they'll share), and a hidden sympathy for the insurgents.

  • Shura (KLE) — sit with the elder. Raises attitude and cooperation and may yield intel. Costs a phase.
  • CERP Projects — spend reconstruction funds on a well, a school, a clinic, a road. Builds lasting goodwill and reduces sympathy.
  • Solatia — after you cause harm, payment and apology can repair a relationship. Offered through events.
Civilian casualties are catastrophic
A single civilian killed in your crossfire or by your fire mission hardens every village in the valley, raises enemy activity, and shakes battalion's confidence. Positively identify your targets. When civilians are near the fight, the cheapest round is the one you don't fire.

9. Intelligence

The Intel Feed aggregates reporting, each tagged with a source and a reliability percentage:

  • SIGINT intercepted "ICOM chatter" — the enemy's radios. Often cryptic, sometimes a warning.
  • HUMINT tips from cooperative villagers and paid sources. Quality tracks the village's cooperation — and sometimes settles a private score.
  • DRONE ISR sightings, especially of night movement.
  • PATROL what your own boots saw.

Intel marks hotspots on the map and raises contact probability nearby. It is frequently wrong. Corroborate across sources before you act on it.

10. Tactical Combat

When contact is made you'll see a briefing, then deploy into the tactical layer: the same terrain, zoomed in, every soldier under your command. Time runs in real time; pause freely and give orders deliberately. The clock, speed controls, friendly/contact counts, and "End Contact" are along the top. Your soldiers act with autonomy within your intent — they return fire, take cover, reload, and the medic moves to the wounded — but the decisions that matter are yours.

12. Terrain, Line of Sight & Concealment

The valley is procedurally generated from your seed: a meandering river floor rising to ridgelines, cut by spurs and draws, with terraced fields, orchards, scrub, forest, scree, and rock. Terrain is not scenery — it is the dominant actor.

  • Elevation drives line of sight. A man behind a crest is in dead ground (defilade); only what pokes above the horizon line can be seen or hit. Partial exposure — "heads and shoulders over the ridge" — is computed smoothly, not as a binary.
  • Concealment (forest, orchard, scrub, smoke) blocks sight without stopping bullets. You can't shoot what you can't see — and neither can he.
  • Cover (rock, qalat walls, terrace walls) stops rounds. Going prone or crouching adds cover and shrinks your silhouette.
  • Movement cost punishes steep slopes, scree, and brush. The climb itself is an enemy: fatigue degrades everything.
Read the ground
Take the high ground. Avoid the obvious draws and trails where ambushes wait. Use dead ground to move unseen. Put a base of fire where it can see, and maneuver where it can't.

13. Ballistics — Every Round Simulated

There is no abstract "to-hit roll." Each shot spawns a real projectile with the weapon's muzzle velocity and time of flight. The aimpoint is the target perturbed by a dispersion drawn from the weapon's mechanical accuracy combined with the shooter's marksmanship, stance, fatigue, movement, suppression, and how settled the shot is. The round flies; on arrival it strikes the target only if it lands within the target's effective silhouette — which shrinks with cover and defilade — and cover may still stop an otherwise-good round depending on the weapon's penetration.

Consequences fall out of the model rather than being scripted: rifles are deadly inside their effective range and waste rounds beyond it; machine guns hit through volume and suppress whole positions; the .50 and DShK defeat most cover; snipers and DMRs reach far but fire slowly. Rounds passing close overhead suppress without hitting — the "crack" that pins men down.

14. Morale, Suppression & Wounds

Each combatant carries composure (morale in the moment) and accrues suppression from incoming fire. Suppressed soldiers shoot wildly, hesitate, and seek cover; broken soldiers cower or rout. Leaders project composure to those around them — lose a leader and his squad wavers. Cohesion (friends nearby) and cover restore composure; isolation, casualties, and fatigue drain it.

Hits roll a body region and a severity, mitigated by body armor (your soldiers wear plates and helmets; the enemy does not). Outcomes range from a graze to a mortal wound. Wounded soldiers bleed; buddy aid slows it, a medic stops it, and MEDEVAC takes them off the field — converting a probable KIA into a WIA who may return to duty.

15. Fire Support

From the Fire Support panel, select an asset and click the impact point:

  • Mortars (60/81mm) — your organic indirect fire from the COP, if the target is in range and rounds remain. There is a delay while the mission is plotted and fired; rounds land with realistic dispersion.
  • CAS / CCA — close air support (a gun run) or a Hellfire, available only when the weather allows aircraft. Devastating, slow to arrive, and limited.
  • MEDEVAC — select a casualty and call the bird to take them off the field.
Danger Close
Fragments do not pick sides. The game warns you when friendlies are within danger-close of an impact, but it will still fire if you order it. Account for the dispersion. Account for civilians.

17. Knowing the Enemy

The insurgent AI fights like the real thing did. He prefers the high ground and defilade, holds his fire until the patrol is in the kill zone, and masses RPG and PKM fire at the moment of initiation. Then he shoots and scoots — fires for a spell and displaces to a new firing position before your mortars range him. He breaks contact when he's hurt, when he's isolated, or when aircraft arrive, slipping up the draws and off the map. He fights hardest near his own villages. He melds into the population. His skill and numbers scale with the "heat" of the valley, which you raise by causing civilian harm and lose by killing fighters and winning villages.

18. Scoring the Tour

There is no simple win. Your deployment is graded across five metrics:

  • Valley Stability — the overall trend, blended from attitudes, enemy strength, and your presence.
  • Village Attitudes — the mean disposition of the population toward you.
  • Enemy Strength — the estimated insurgent pool; attrition lowers it, your mistakes feed it.
  • Combat Power — your readiness in men and materiel.
  • Higher Confidence — battalion's faith in you. Hit zero and you're relieved of command.

At the end of the tour you receive a letter grade and an honest assessment — and a list of your killed, by name and home state. The rarest grade of all is the one where everyone goes home.
