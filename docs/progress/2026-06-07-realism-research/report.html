In the Mountains · field report

Soldier-Scale Realism

Four things that “don’t feel quite right” — diagnosed in the code, measured headless, and checked against the field manuals. A brief for the next campaign.
2026-06-07 · research only · companion plan: PLAN-soldier-scale-realism.md
No code was changed. A parallel scale campaign owns the working tree. This report and its plan are preparation: every claim is pinned to a file:line, a measured number, or a cited manual.

The owner flagged four complaints while watching the game at close zoom. Read together, they aren’t four bugs — they’re one story.

The thesis: a resolution mismatch. The simulation is tuned for the operational scale — a 2.56 km valley, multi-hour patrols, 5 m terrain cells. But the player now watches at the soldier scale, and at that scale the seams alias: a 5-minute census (temporal), a squad that can’t use a rock or switchback a slope (spatial), and an on-contact drill that only ever fixes or runs (doctrinal). Fix the resolution — in time, in space, and in the AI’s tactical vocabulary — and all four resolve.
The four items
  1. Village dwell is ~70× too short — and the census is instant
  2. The squad fixes in place or runs — it never flanks
  3. A 5 m cover raster erases the rock he’d hide behind
  4. Squads ring the spur instead of climbing the mountain
  5. Synthesis — the two-tier terrain, and a sequence

How this was verified. Three independent passes, kept separate so they could disagree: (1) a close read of the engine with every mechanism quoted at its file:line; (2) purpose-built headless probes that turn each complaint into a number on the real, deterministic sim; (3) doctrine research against the US Army field manuals and first-hand Korengal accounts. Where the probe contradicted the hypothesis, the probe won — see item 2.

01Village dwell is ~70× too short

“Squads show up at a village, do a census, stick around a very short time, then leave. The time they spend doesn’t feel right — and what other activities are too quick?” — owner, on watching a census

The mechanism. Every on-station hold reads one function, dwellFor() in lib/sim/world/helpers.ts:30. It is a flat table of game-seconds:

Activitycoded dwell= game-minon screen @4×realistic
Census / cordon-search300 s575 s4–10 h
KLE / shura360 s690 s1–3 h (cumulative)
Establish OP / overwatch900 s15225 s3–8 h (24 h cap)
Ambush1100 s18275 shours (8 h rotate)
Recon150 s2.538 s≥ 1 h
Presence (default)180 s345 shours · 3×/day

At the default 4× speed (state/store.ts:341; speeds are [1,2,4,8,16]) a 5-minute census is gone in 75 real seconds; at 16× in ~19 s. Worse, the census effect is instantaneous: the moment the squad is near the village, tasks.ts:443 flips censusDone = true and calls advanceCensus() — the dwell that follows is a static wait, not work being done.

What reality says. A cordon-and-search is a half-to-full-day operation built from a four-element task organisation (cordon / search / support / command), worked qalat-by-qalat — and biometric enrollment alone runs up to 30 minutes per person (CALL Commander’s Guide to Biometrics). A shura is 1–3 hours, but its real power is cumulative across a weekly cadence over months — one cup of tea changes nothing. An ambush is mostly patient hours of waiting (8-hour rotation), with contact measured in seconds. The classic disappointment to model is Restrepo’s “Cow Incident”: the long-awaited elder-initiated shura turned out to be a demand for compensation for a cow killed in the wire.

Recommendation

  • Raise dwell toward reality, driven by population — census ≈ 2–8 h scaled by fighting-age residents (the enrollment clock), KLE ≈ 1–2 h, OP ≈ 3–8 h, recon ≥ 1 h.
  • Make the census progressive, not instant — censusDone flips only after each compound is worked / each person enrolled; an early recall yields a partial census.
  • Add a dwell event-roll so a long, realistic hold surfaces decisions — a find, a biometric hit, a tip, an elder grievance, a med moment, an FET gap, a squirter — reusing the existing PendingEvent.
  • Let warp be the way you pass it — skip-to-event already stops on what matters; long dwell becomes tension, not tedium. The fix is longer + meaningful + warp-through, never just bigger numbers.
Sources: FM 3-06.11 Ch.14 (cordon-and-search); CALL Commander’s Guide to Biometrics in Afghanistan (≤30 min/person); FM 7-8 / ATP 3-21.8 (24 h patrol-base cap, 8 h ambush rotation); Junger/Hetherington Restrepo (Cow Incident). Code: helpers.ts:30, tasks.ts:443, store.ts:341.

02The squad fixes in place or runs — it never flanks

“In the firefights I’ve seen, the whole squad sits back and sets up a base of fire, and one squad member moves forward. That’s immersion-breaking — it’s not how squad tactics work.” — owner, on watching a contact

The mechanism. A squad’s on-contact behaviour is set by its SOP. The defaults (types.ts:67) are contact:"hold" for census/presence/cordon and "suppress" for overwatch/ambush — never "assault". Both hold and suppress route to stampBaseOfFire() (squad-combat.ts:145), which puts every man on a base of fire. A maneuver element is split off only in the assault path (assignElements, squad-combat.ts:357) — so the squad flanks only if the player hand-picks “Assault.” And even then it beelines at the threat centroid (orderTarget = threat, squad-combat.ts:178) — a frontal rush, not a covered flank.

What the probe found — and where it corrected me. I drove squads into contact and sampled, every half-second, how many men were maneuvering — at two tempos. The first surprise: the chosen SOP barely matters. Hold, Suppress, and Assault produced nearly identical behaviour, because an automatic break-contact safety (squad-combat.ts:72) overrides the SOP whenever the squad is overmatched. The squad has effectively two modes — fix-in-place and run — and no offensive middle.

Light contact · the owner’s regime

The squad stays holding 92% of contact time. While fixed, a mean of ~3 of 9 men move (median 2) — and those are individual cover-seeks and buddy-aid, not a maneuver element. The rest hold a base of fire. This is precisely “the whole squad sits, a couple shuffle.”

Hot contact · overmatched

The break-safety fires: the squad spends ~60–77% of contact time breaking contact — most of the squad leapfrogging rearward (mean ~6 men moving at once). It runs; it doesn’t close.

So the owner’s “one man moving” is real — it lives in the light-contact regime, where stampBaseOfFire roots everyone and only seekCover shuffles individuals. But the deeper finding is what’s missing from both regimes, and from all three SOP settings: an autonomous flank assault. The squad either sits or runs. It never fixes-with-one-team-and-maneuvers-with-the-other — the one thing a rifle squad is built to do.

What reality says. A rifle squad is built as two fire teams precisely so one suppresses while the other moves. The maneuver element is a whole fire team (~4 men), never a lone rifleman; it moves to a vulnerable flank via a covered route (often peeling back first, then swinging wide) and closes by 3–5-second buddy-team rushes as the base of fire shifts. A frontal rush at the centroid is doctrinally wrong. And crucially, battle drills trigger without an order — a squad that only ever holds until told to maneuver has failed the most basic infantry standard.

Recommendation

  • Give the squad-leader AI the maneuver decision as one gate in squadFight: react → assess fire superiority → if achievable and a covered flank exists, assault via the flank; else hold/suppress; else break. The player’s SOP becomes guidance/permission, not the on/off switch for maneuvering. (This is the design soul: “combat is 100% AI; the hardest part of command is watching.”)
  • Route the maneuver team to a flank, not the centroid — reuse the engine’s cover/conceal-biased findPath to the enemy’s flank.
  • Model bounding overwatch — split the maneuver team into buddy pairs; only one moves at a time (3–5 s rushes); smoke screens each bound (already modeled).
  • Don’t add soldier-level micro — the player still only sets SOP and approves fires. Keep the frontal assault as the fallback when no covered flank exists.
Sources: ATP/FM 3-21.8 Battle Drills 2A (squad assault) & 3 (break contact); FM 7-8 Ch.4. Code: types.ts:67, squad-combat.ts:72,145,178,357. Probe: tools/gen-combat-movers.ts.

03A 5 m cover raster erases the rock he’d hide behind

“Down at the size of a soldier it’s hard to tell what’s going on. I’d like to see a man go behind a rock, or a low wall, or a small berm — micro-terrain that soldiers use, especially in combat.” — owner, on close-zoom terrain

The mechanism. Cover and concealment are a single averaged number per 5 m cell (COVER_CONCEAL, terrain.ts:2614; sampled by coverAt, :2480). The Land enum already names the right features — TerraceWall, Boulders, DryWash, CompoundWall — but only as 5×5 m blobs. Meanwhile the rocks and trees you see are, by their own file’s admission, a “Pure render overlay (no sim impact)” (lib/render/decoration.ts:13): the drawn rock is decoupled from the cover field, so a soldier can’t actually get behind that rock. A real rock is ~0.5 m; a 5 m cell is 100× its footprint — the features that decide a firefight are sub-cell and average out.

What reality says. Cover (stops bullets) and concealment (blocks sight) are different things, and “even the smallest depression or fold in the ground can give some cover” (FM 21-75). The Korengal was fought from terrace walls, sangars, rock outcrops, and the draws — at COP Restrepo the crew arranged their cots against the one wall “that couldn’t get hit.” Every serious wargame solves this the same way: model discrete cover objects, not a cover raster. Combat Mission uses walls and foxholes as objects with posture-dependent cover (a low wall is partial standing, full cover prone); VBS uses placed “biotopes” with round penetration. The elevation grid carries terrain form; cover lives in a separate object layer.

Recommendation

  • Add a discrete cover-object layer — boulders, low rock/terrace walls, qalat walls, sangars, berms, fighting positions — each with frontage, height, and a material→stop-probability. Generate them deterministically from the landcover (stable-hash scatter, like the decoration layer).
  • Make cover posture-dependent — partial standing, full prone behind a low wall; behind a boulder ≈ foxhole-grade; open ground stays exposed. Wire into the existing stance behaviour.
  • Have the combat AI snap to the nearest cover object on contact (seekCover/findCover query objects, not just the raster), and render the object the engine actually uses — no more cosmetic decoupling.
Sources: FM 21-75 (cover vs concealment); ATP 3-21.8 (defilade/dead space); Combat Mission (action spots + wall/foxhole objects, exposure %); VBS biotopes. Code: terrain.ts:2480,2614, decoration.ts:13.

04Squads ring the spur instead of climbing the mountain

“I told a squad to establish an OP up the nearby mountain. I expected them to go straight up — slowly, zigzagging if they had to. Instead they took the road all the way around the spur and went up the shallowest ridge on the far side of a draw. That feels wrong.” — owner, on an Establish-OP order

The mechanism, with a number. Foot-movement cost is isotropic: moveCostAt = clamp(landMult · (1 − slope·0.62) …) (terrain.ts:2586) reads only the cell’s slope magnitude — identical in every heading. So a switchback only adds distance and never pays off. And slope > 1.25 (≈51°) is hard-impassable (terrain.ts:2581), while slope is a forward-difference over 5 m — so procedural roughness scatters “cliff” cells across a climbable face, fragmenting it into fake cliffs. The planner’s only cheap option is the long way around.

I rendered the planner’s actual output for an “Establish OP” order against the real generated terrain (tools/gen-oproute.ts, seed korengal-2):

443 m → 3,060 m = ×6.91 detour
46% of the straight-line ascent is flagged impassable (mean slope 1.04 rise/run). And the road discount is nearly irrelevant — the roadBias 0.55 and roadBias 0 routes are essentially identical, so the detour is driven by the slope cutoff + isotropic cost, not road preference.

What reality says. Human walking speed and energy depend on the signed grade along the direction of travel — Naismith’s rule, Tobler’s hiking function W = 6·e^(−3.5·|S+0.05|), Minetti’s metabolic curve — so cost is intrinsically anisotropic. The GIS literature is explicit: an isotropic per-cell cost mathematically cannot produce switchbacks (a diagonal traverse “buys nothing”), while an anisotropic cost makes the diagonal cheaper and switchbacks emerge above a ~25% critical slope. Mountain doctrine (ATTP 3-21.50) ascends steep ground by diagonal traverse / switchback and avoids the canalized road — the predictable, observed approach, exactly where the Korengal ambushes were sprung. The game does the doctrinally-wrong thing (the road) for the wrong reason (a cost model that can’t zigzag).

Recommendation

  • Replace the isotropic cost with a Tobler-based, signed-grade (anisotropic) step cost in the A* edge evaluation (path.ts:309) — the single change that makes switchbacks emerge.
  • Soften the foot impassability cutoff — infantry climb steeper than 51°, slowly; add a climbable-but-very-slow band so a steep face has a through-line, and compute/smooth slope at the tactical resolution so 5 m roughness stops faking cliffs.
  • Plan tactical legs on a finer, better-connected grid (~5 m or finer, ≥16-direction or any-angle Theta*) so a shallow traverse is representable, with a turn penalty so switchbacks are few-bend, not jitter.
  • Target: the worst-case detour drops from ×6.91 toward ≈ ×1.2–1.6, with a visible zigzag climb — proven on held-out seeds, without regressing cross-valley routing.
Sources: Tobler’s hiking function; Naismith’s rule; Minetti et al. 2002; Herzog (critical slope ~25%); Crowe/NPS (grid-heading limits on switchbacks); ATTP 3-21.50 (mountain movement). Code: terrain.ts:2581,2586, path.ts:309,355, formation.ts:90. Probe: tools/gen-oproute.ts.

★Synthesis — one foundation, a clear sequence

Items 3 and 4 share the same cheapest fix, so they should be built on one foundation rather than as two unrelated efforts:

Don’t

Raise the whole 512² valley to 1 m resolution. That’s 25× the cells (6.5 million), a memory and determinism budget blow-out, for detail you only need where soldiers actually are.

Do — two-tier terrain

Keep the 5 m grid as the operational layer (strategic map, cross-valley routing). Instantiate a ~1 m tactical patch — discrete cover objects + anisotropic cost — only around active units. Build it once; items 3 and 4 both consume it.

Recommended sequence. (1) Item 1 first — small, no architecture, high felt-impact. (2) Item 2 — AI-only, the squad-leader maneuver gate. (3) The two-tier terrain foundation. (4) Items 3 and 4 on top of it. Hold items 3 and 4 until the in-flight scale campaign lands — they depend on the final cell size and elevation field. Full task breakdown, with per-workstream Definitions-of-Done and verification harnesses, is in PLAN-soldier-scale-realism.md.

The bar this aims at: a soldier reading a field manual watches a squad take contact, fix the enemy with one team, flank with the other along a covered draw, tuck behind a terrace wall, and — when ordered to the high ground — switchback up the face instead of strolling the road around it… and says “holy shit, an AI built this.”
